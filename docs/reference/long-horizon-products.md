# What could sqrlly make possible in ten years?

Experimental branch study, 2026-11-22.

The four [applied workflows](workflow-use-case-experiment.md) ask what one team can produce, from an app to a manuscript. A different question is what several parties could do together if each could inspect the evidence, challenge a rule, and decline to act. My highest-upside bet is **a member-owned exchange for spare capacity**. My first investment would be much smaller. Give one operations lead a way to answer “who would lose service under this proposed change?” before putting the change into effect.

These are product hypotheses. The existing runner can execute local comparisons, preserve outputs, and stop a dependent step. A customer has yet to demonstrate a willingness to pay for any of the products below. The pilots have deliberately different buyers, success measures, and reasons to walk away.

## 1. Rehearse changes to the rules an organization lives by

Consider a library revising its transfer rules. A branch can improve its agreement with reviewers across all requests while serving fewer readers who lack local access. An operations lead needs the list of affected requests, the size of each denominator, and a way to contest the examples. The person proposing a change has a reason to use this at the *next* rule revision, even if the organization never adopts AI.

Existing tools set a high floor. Open Policy Agent can record the input, policy and bundle metadata for a decision. LangSmith can compare application versions against a reference dataset. The opportunity for sqrlly is to let the people who own a service run its actual decision procedure, including existing scripts or model-assisted steps, against a frozen history and decide whether to adopt it. Rebuilding an evaluation dashboard buys little. [Open Policy Agent decision logs](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/management-decision-logs), [LangSmith evaluation types](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/evaluation-types)

**First paid pilot.** Ask one operations lead for the last disputed rule change and roughly 100 de-identified cases. Preserve what was knowable at the time. Keep the proposed rule blind to later adjudications. Have two reviewers independently inspect the disagreements and record unresolved ones. Run the current review process on the same change. If the replay surfaces a material case the team missed, have them shadow the next change for a week while staff continue to make the actual decisions. Measure reviewer time and ask which rule they chose and why. Stop if the team would have reached the same decision from its existing checks at lower cost, or if it cannot reconstruct the original inputs.

Over several years, an institution could retain enough of this history to revisit its own rules after staff turnover. A person questioning a decision could see the rule in force on the day, correct an input, and request reconsideration by someone with authority. That would require an appeals process, access controls, and a way to record a human reversal. Replaying a rule alone cannot establish that the rule was justified.

## 2. Give independent organizations a way to pool capacity

A tool-lending cooperative can have both a waitlist and idle equipment. Each shop knows the condition of its equipment and what its own customers were promised. A shared spreadsheet can advertise inventory. The harder question is whether the shops will honor a shared reservation when their local needs change. Members could rehearse the proposed allocation rule at home, share their decisions, and reserve one item only after the required parties agree.

Libraries are a good place to learn and a crowded place to sell. OCLC already automates interlibrary borrowing and lending. A better first buyer might be a cooperative of repair shops with an existing dispatcher and a shared piece of diagnostic equipment. That dispatcher already knows which jobs someone lost because another member's inventory was invisible. The parties who supply the equipment should set the allocation and withdrawal rules. Existing cooperatives offer a model for that ownership. [OCLC WorldShare Interlibrary Loan](https://www.oclc.org/en/worldshare-ill.html), [International Cooperative Alliance principles](https://www.ica.coop/en/cooperatives/cooperative-identity)

**Field test.** Ask three members to contribute four weeks of past requests for one class of equipment. Run the same rule separately at each shop. A member may withhold consent even when its local checks pass. Start by exchanging only approved decisions, then ask the dispatcher to arrange five reversible bookings using the current phone or email process. Count *fulfilled* bookings, late returns, member refusals, and coordination time. Walk away if every match still requires the dispatcher to negotiate the rules from scratch, or a member would rather keep its capacity private at the offered price.

If that works, the next step is expiring reservations and receipts from both sides. A failed handoff needs a named owner and an explicit release of the reservation. Two local database commits cannot guarantee a physical handoff. In another industry, the unit might be furnace time or surplus heat. Kalundborg already demonstrates real firms exchanging water, energy, and materials. Physical links, compatible specifications, distance, and counterparties determine which trades are possible. Software has a claim only on the coordination cost of exchanges the parties can actually fulfill. [Kalundborg industrial exchanges](https://www.symbiosis.dk/en/okosystem-bag-kalundborg-symbiose/)

Running code where the data lives is already possible. PySyft, for instance, lets a data owner review a submitted computation and release results. A cooperative needs something beyond that capability. Its members must agree on who can reserve the resource, how long that promise lasts, and who bears a loss if the promise is broken. The current sqrlly runner executes locally supplied commands and offers no isolation for an untrusted partner's code. [OpenMined's owner review workflow](https://openmined.org/pysyft/)

The ten-year possibility is a network whose members own the operating rules and can replace its software provider. A regional association could collect dues and pay for hosting, reconciliation, and support. Each new member brings a resource other members might need. The cost is governance. If an incumbent broker already clears the available supply efficiently, adding members to a second network creates little value.

## 3. Buy a verified result from an open field of suppliers

A city archives office wants a searchable collection of scanned meeting minutes. Before inviting bids, it could specify the required output, define how errors in names and dates count, and reserve unseen pages for acceptance. A two-person firm could demonstrate performance on the buyer's equipment. A second verifier could reproduce the result. The buyer would have a means to compare bids from firms it has never hired.

NASA's prize competitions show an institution soliciting solutions against a challenge. ACM SIGMOD's reproducibility program shows the work involved when an independent reviewer reruns an artifact. A buyer could use both ideas for routine purchasing. The scarce asset would be a catalogue of acceptance procedures buyers and suppliers both trust. A repository of seller-supplied success logs would have much less value. [NASA past challenges](https://www.nasa.gov/prizes-challenges-and-crowdsourcing/centennial-challenges/past-challenges/), [ACM SIGMOD reproducibility](https://reproducibility.sigmodconf.hosting.acm.org/index.html)

**Cheapest test.** Take one completed purchase, remove the supplier identities, and have two people independently run the acceptance procedure on two submissions on clean hosts. Include one submission that passes the public examples and mishandles an important withheld case. Record setup time, verifier disagreement, the cost of defining “accepted,” and whether the buyer would put the procedure in the next solicitation. Stop if resolving disputes costs more than the buyer's present review. A sustained service would need buyer-controlled holdouts, a sandbox for submitted code, and an appeal for criteria the buyer left ambiguous. Actual contracting and payment would stay with the buyer until an institution chooses to delegate them.

## 4. Transfer judgment between generations of practitioners

A senior repair technician often knows which job needs a second inspection. That knowledge disappears if the work order records only the eventual repair. An apprentice confronted with a rare case needs the observations, alternatives the technician considered, and the evidence that changed the decision. Two skilled people can reasonably disagree. Preserving both readings gives the next apprentice something a checklist cannot supply.

Microsoft already documents recording task variants and analyzing their steps. A possible product for trade associations is a collection of difficult cases, with a practical examination for apprentices and a credit or fee for the practitioners who contribute and update them. The reviewer can retire a case when equipment changes and retain why it used to matter. [Microsoft task mining](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-automate/task-mining-tutorial)

**Cheapest test.** Ask two technicians in a refurbishment shop to independently explain three borderline jobs. Use those demonstrations to prepare exercises. Give apprentices twenty unseen historical jobs, randomize which learning aid they use, and have a third technician grade escalation and reasoning blind. Track time taken away from billable work as well as missed escalations. Drop the idea if the existing checklist performs as well, the experts will not contribute another case, or a grader cannot distinguish sound reasoning from fortunate guesses. The first product here would be training the shop can buy. Organizational memory emerges only if someone keeps contributing after the initial workshop.

## 5. Run a research program that chooses its next physical experiment

A small acoustic-panel manufacturer has a fixed budget for testing material thicknesses and fiber blends. Its annual question is which panel to make. A useful system would read the measurements so far, budget the remaining tests, request the next sample from a technician, and revise the plan when measurements conflict. A long-lived study could preserve dead ends, calibration changes, and the reason to stop. Producing a paper would be one possible output.

Ax already provides adaptive trial selection and accepts measured results. sqrlly could orchestrate the parts around such a library, including materials inventory, a technician's acceptance of the proposed run, provenance for each measurement, and a spending limit across batches. The test of value is a better decision per unit of laboratory time. [Ax adaptive experimentation](https://ax.dev/)

**Cheapest test.** Start with a completed panel study that measured a grid of configurations. Reveal one historical measurement at a time within a fixed budget. Compare the adaptive plan with the lab's actual ordering and a random ordering over multiple starts. Hide a separate batch for confirmation. Then ask a technician to conduct one prospective batch and compare measured performance, cost, and repeats against an agreed plan. Stop if a simple fixed design is as effective or the apparent gain disappears across batches. The replay can only score settings with recorded measurements. Autonomous equipment control would be a different investment with its own acceptance criteria.

## What to build and when to change course

| Horizon | Investment | Evidence that earns the next investment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 90 days | One service team, one contested rule, one protected service measure. Keep the replay local so the team controls its case history. | The case-level comparison changes a real release decision. A second rule owner asks to use it. |
| Within a year | Let two independent sites review one proposal, run on their own cases, compare conclusions, and withhold assent. Record the cost of making the evidence reviewable. | The second site runs without the workflow author. Both parties can explain a disagreement and accept the process used to settle it. |
| Years 2–3 | Choose between a service for organizations changing rules and a cooperative coordinating actual capacity. Budget for the latter only if a dispatcher and resource owners join. | Members make and fulfill repeat reservations. Reconciled records show what happened after a refusal, cancellation, or late return. |
| Years 4–10 | Let another cooperative adopt the same exchange procedure under different local terms. Give members a way to export commitments and replace the operator. Pursue procurement, apprenticeship, or laboratory work as separate businesses if their pilots have buyers. | A second network operates independently. Renewal, fulfilled exchanges, and operator replacement show whether shared rules have lasting value. |

## What you can reproduce on this branch

The [library replay](../../examples/experimental/policy_replay/README.md) has 11 synthetic requests and nine fixture adjudications. One proposed rule increases agreement from 5/9 to 7/9 while reducing recommended shipments from 2/3 to 1/3 among reviewed priority requests marked for shipment. The workflow holds that change and preserves the affected IDs. Another rule reaches 9/9 agreement and 3/3 on that group. Two requests have no adjudication. Future deliveries and changes to inventory remain unknown. The rules and fixtures are visible together, so this is a test of the machinery on known examples.

The [two-library rehearsal](../../examples/experimental/policy_replay/README.md#two-library-consent-rehearsal) adds a second cohort. West improves from 3/5 to 5/5 on its reviewed cases. Each member issues a separate decision for the same round, rules, and evaluator. Even after both local checks pass, West can withhold. The coordinator then stops and identifies the dissenting member. Given both approvals, it proceeds. Only the small decision files go to the coordinator. The exercise uses a trusted local host and relies on the operator to supply each member's current decision. It leaves identity verification, withdrawal after collection, and fulfillment for a field pilot.

The largest uncertainty is whether independent parties will commit a resource under rules they can decline. Only the parties themselves, making and fulfilling a real commitment, can answer it.
